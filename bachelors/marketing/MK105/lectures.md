# MK105: Statistics & Market Research Methods
## Bachelor of Science in Marketing — University of Yggdrasil, 2040

**Credits:** 4  
**Description:** This course equips students with quantitative and qualitative research tools for modern marketing. Students learn experimental design, inferential statistics, predictive modeling, and ethical data collection in a world where AI‑augmented analytics dominate.

**Instructor:** Dr. Freyja Hrafnkelsson, Director of the Yggdrasil Market Insight Lab
**Lab:** Mímir Data Studio, Second Floor, Heimdallr Business Centre
**Office Hours:** Wednesdays 14:00‑16:00, or by appointment

## Lectures

ᚠ **Lecture 1: Foundations of Marketing Measurement — From Børs to Big Data**
### Overview
Marketing measurement has travelled from the mercantile ledgers of the 9th‑century kaupþing to the real‑time data streams of 2040. This lecture surveys the evolution of measurement, the philosophy of quantification, and the contemporary distinction between descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics. Students examine classic works such as *The Wealth of Nations* (Smith, 1776) and the 2040 *Nordic Data Transparency Act*, mapping how regulatory shifts shape what can be measured.

### Required Reading
- Smith, A. *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) – Chapter 5 (Commerce and Statistics).
- Yggdrasil Data Transparency Act (2040), §§2‑4, available at https://yggdrasil.gov/legislation/dta2040
- Hargreaves, J. (2029). *From Ledgers to Lakes: The Data Evolution*, Journal of Marketing History, 12(3), 45‑68.

### Discussion Questions
1. How do cultural notions of privacy in Nordic societies influence the design of market measurement systems?
2. In what ways can over‑reliance on quantitative metrics obscure qualitative brand equity?
3. Compare the ethical implications of tracking consumer behavior via QR‑code coupons in 2025 vs. AI‑driven neuro‑preference models in 2040.

---

ᚢ **Lecture 2: Survey Design in the Age of Adaptive AI**
### Overview
Surveys remain a cornerstone of market research, but modern platforms deploy adaptive question routing powered by reinforcement learning. This lecture examines construct validity, sampling strategies, and the algorithmic personalization of question flow. Students will critique the 2040 *Adaptive Survey Framework* (ASF) and implement a simple Python‑based adaptive script using the open‑source *Bifrost* library.

### Required Reading
- Dillman, D. et al. (2022). *Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed‑Mode Surveys* (4th ed.). Sage. Chapter 7.
- Adaptive Survey Framework (ASF) Specification (2040), https://github.com/yggdrasil/ASF
- Nielsen, L. (2035). "Algorithmic Bias in Adaptive Surveys," *Marketing Science Review*, 28(2), 112‑129.

### Discussion Questions
1. What safeguards are necessary to prevent reinforcement‑learning driven surveys from reinforcing existing biases?
2. How does the ASF balance respondent fatigue against data richness?
3. Propose a method for auditing an adaptive survey’s decision tree for compliance with the Huginn Standards.

---

ᚦ **Lecture 3: Experimental Design — Causal Inference for Marketers**
### Overview
Understanding causality is crucial for allocating marketing spend. This lecture covers A/B testing, multivariate testing, and newer causal inference techniques such as Double Machine Learning (DML) and Bayesian hierarchical models. Case studies include the 2038 *Bifrost A/B Platform* roll‑out at a major streaming service.

### Required Reading
- Kohavi, R. et al. (2020). *Online Controlled Experiments*, Cambridge University Press. Chapters 2‑4.
- Rubin, D. (2021). *Causal Inference in Marketing*, Marketing Analytics Journal, 15(1), 1‑24.
- Yggdrasil Bifrost A/B Platform Documentation (2038), https://bifrost.yggdrasil.io/docs

### Discussion Questions
1. Discuss the trade‑offs between frequentist p‑value thresholds and Bayesian credible intervals in high‑velocity test environments.
2. How can the “Muspell Effect” of many small, simultaneous tests lead to macro‑level market distortions?
3. Design an experiment to test the impact of a new AI‑generated ad copy on click‑through rates, accounting for network effects.

---

ᚨ **Lecture 4: Regression and Predictive Modeling for Market Segmentation**
### Overview
Linear regression, logistic regression, and tree‑based methods remain foundational, but 2040 marketers augment them with transformer‑based embeddings of unstructured data. This lecture walks through data preparation, feature engineering (including neuro‑preference vectors), model selection, and evaluation metrics (AIC, BIC, ROC‑AUC). Students will build a segmentation model using the open‑source *Odin* library.

### Required Reading
- James, G. et al. (2020). *An Introduction to Statistical Learning* (2nd ed.). Springer. Chapter 3.
- Sørensen, K. (2037). *Transformer Embeddings for Consumer Insight*, *International Journal of Marketing Analytics*, 9(4), 201‑219.
- Odin Library Documentation (2040), https://github.com/yggdrasil/odin

### Discussion Questions
1. How do transformer embeddings change the interpretability of traditional segmentation models?
2. Evaluate the ethical considerations of using neuro‑preference data for micro‑targeting.
3. Propose a validation strategy for a segmentation model that must operate across multiple Nordic markets with differing privacy regulations.

ᚱ **Lecture 5: Qualitative Research — Ethnography, Interviews, and Digital Shadowing**
### Overview
Quantitative rigor must be balanced with deep qualitative insights. This lecture introduces participant observation, semi‑structured interviewing, and the emerging practice of “digital shadowing” via consented AI agents that follow consumers in virtual environments. Students analyze the 2039 *Nordic Ethnographic Code* and apply thematic analysis using the *Saga* software suite.

### Required Reading
- Spradley, J. (2021). *The Ethnographic Interview* (2nd ed.). Sage.
- Nordic Ethnographic Code (2039), https://ethics.nordic.gov/NEC2039
- Saga Software User Guide (2040), https://saga.yggdrasil.io/manual

### Discussion Questions
1. In what ways can AI‑mediated digital shadowing enhance or undermine the authenticity of ethnographic findings?
2. Discuss the balance between ecological validity and participant privacy in the context of the Nordics’ strong data protection culture.
3. How can findings from a small‑scale ethnography be responsibly generalized to a national market segment?

ᚹ **Lecture 6: Sampling Strategies for a Hyper‑Connected Consumer Base**
### Overview
Traditional probability sampling faces challenges when the population is fragmented across platforms, IoT devices, and AI agents. This lecture explores stratified, cluster, and adaptive sampling techniques, including the use of synthetic populations generated by agent‑based models. Case study: the 2040 *Yggdrasil Synthetic Consumer Atlas*.

### Required Reading
- Lohr, S. (2019). *Sampling: Design and Analysis* (2nd ed.). Brooks/Cole. Chapter 5.
- Yggdrasil Synthetic Consumer Atlas (2040), https://data.yggdrasil.io/atlas
- Chen, H. (2038). "Agent‑Based Sampling for AI‑Mediated Markets," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 56(3), 321‑340.

### Discussion Questions
1. How does the creation of a synthetic population affect the external validity of market research findings?
2. Evaluate the trade‑offs between cost, speed, and accuracy when employing adaptive sampling in a live e‑commerce environment.
3. Propose a sampling plan for a new AI‑driven product launch targeting Gen‑Z consumers in Iceland.

ᚦ **Lecture 8: Advanced Multivariate Techniques — Factor Analysis, SEM, and Causal Modeling**
### Overview
Multivariate analysis enables marketers to uncover latent constructs and model complex relationships. This lecture covers exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling (SEM), and recent advances in causal graph learning (e.g., PC algorithm, Do‑Calculus). Students will replicate a 2029 *Brand Equity* model using the *Valkyrie* R package.

### Required Reading
- Hair, J.F. et al. (2021). *Multivariate Data Analysis* (8th ed.). Pearson. Chapters 10‑12.
- Valkyrie Package V2.0 (2029), https://cran.r‑project.org/package=valkyrie
- Pearl, J. (2020). *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press. Chapter 4.

### Discussion Questions
1. Discuss the interpretive differences between a reflective and a formative measurement model in the context of brand equity.
2. How can causal graph learning be applied to disentangle the impact of AI‑generated ad exposure from organic brand sentiment?
3. Propose a validation strategy for an SEM model that includes both survey‑based latent variables and system‑generated behavioral metrics.

ᚾ **Lecture 9: Ethics, Privacy, and the Huginn Standards**

Research ethics have become more complex as data sources proliferate. This lecture reviews informed consent, data minimization, and the Huginn Standards for AI‑mediated research. ### Required Reading

### Discussion Questions

2. Analyze the tension between the “right to explanation” and proprietary AI models used in predictive marketing analytics.
3. Draft a consent form snippet for a study that records eye‑tracking data during ad exposure.

This lecture covers model selection, cross‑validation in non‑stationary environments, and the use of *Jörmungandr* – an open‑source time‑series forecasting library tuned for Nordic retail data.

### Required Reading
- Hyndman, R.J., & Athanasopoulos, G. (2021). *Forecasting: Principles and Practice* (3rd ed.). OTexts. Chapters 7‑9.
- Jörmungandr Library Documentation (2040), https://github.com/yggdrasil/jormungandr
- Svensson, L. (2039). "Ensemble Forecasting for Seasonal Retail in the Nordics," *European Journal of Marketing*, 45(2), 210‑227.

### Discussion Questions
1. How does concept drift manifest in fast‑changing digital marketing environments, and what mitigation strategies are effective?
2. Compare the interpretability of a classical ARIMA model versus a transformer‑based forecast for a seasonal product line.
3. Design a monitoring dashboard for a deployed forecasting model, including alerts for performance degradation.

## Expanded Scholarly Appendix

The rapid evolution of AI-augmented marketing in the Nordic context invites a re-examination of classical persuasive theory through a computational lens. Scholars such as Kjellsson (2041) argue that the integration of transformer-based language models with adaptive survey frameworks creates a feedback loop where audience data not only informs message construction but also reshapes the underlying preference structures. This recursive dynamic challenges the static notion of "ethos" posited by Aristotle, suggesting a distributed moral agency where credibility is co‑constructed by human designers and algorithmic mediators.

In practice, the Bifrǫst Communication Engine deployed at the Yggdrasil Data Transparency Initiative exemplifies this paradigm shift. By continuously monitoring biometric signals—pupil dilation, galvanic skin response, and micro‑expressions—Bifrǫst adjusts rhetorical strategies in real time, aligning pathos with momentary affective states. Yet this capability raises profound ethical questions: does real‑time affective tailoring constitute manipulation, or is it an extension of the age‑old art of tailoring discourse to audience mood? The Huginn Standards stipulate that any adaptive persuasion system must embed an explicit "explainability overlay" that logs decision pathways and provides end‑users with a concise justification for each persuasive adjustment.

Empirical studies conducted between 2042 and 2044 across the Nordic retail sector reveal that transparency‑enhanced adaptive surveys achieve a 12‑115 % increase in completion rates while reducing perceived intrusiveness, provided that participants receive clear consent dialogs and opt‑out mechanisms. Conversely, deployments lacking such safeguards have been associated with heightened public scrutiny and regulatory intervention, as documented in the 2045 Nordic Consumer Protection Report. These findings underscore the importance of aligning technological capability with normative frameworks, a theme that recurs throughout the twelve‑lecture series.

Future research avenues include the development of hybrid causal‑inferential models that can disentangle the influence of AI‑mediated messaging from organic brand equity dynamics, and the exploration of cross‑cultural extensions of the Bifrǫst paradigm into non‑Nordic markets where privacy norms differ markedly.

By engaging with these complexities, students will be equipped not only to harness cutting‑edge analytical tools but also to navigate the ethical terrain that defines responsible 2040‑era marketing practice.
